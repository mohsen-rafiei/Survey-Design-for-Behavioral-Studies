## How to use this checklist

Work through the sections in the order you build the instrument. Each item names a mistake that recurs in the literature, states what to do instead, and gives the source. Tick the box when the item has been dealt with or judged not to apply.

The items are not equally important. Those marked with an asterisk have the strongest or most frequently replicated evidence behind them and should be settled before anything else.

## Part A. Before writing any items: purpose and measurement

- [ ] **\* Writing questions before the information need is defined.** Hold a scoping exercise with co-researchers, agree on exactly which variables the study needs, and establish a clear need for every question. Remove any item that is there because it might be nice to know. (Boynton et al., 2004; Frary, 2020)
- [ ] **Assuming a survey is the right instrument.** Ask whether the information can be observed, logged or taken from records instead. In one quality-improvement collaborative, expert reviewers recommended only 19% of locally written survey items and rejected 46%. (Reed et al., 2024)
- [ ] **\* Modifying an existing scale, or writing a new one, without recording and reporting what was done.** List every item, its source and every change in a measurement plan before data collection, and report validity evidence in the paper. Undisclosed measurement decisions are a large and usually invisible source of researcher degrees of freedom. (Flake et al., 2020)
- [ ] **Reusing a validated scale in a new population without checking that it still works there.** Pretest reused items with the new population. Established instruments produced comprehension failures with justice-involved youth and were judged ambiguous or irrelevant by organizational respondents. (Silva et al., 2019; Einola et al., 2020)
- [ ] **\* Planning to compare groups, conditions or time points on a scale without planning a measurement invariance test.** Write the invariance test into the analysis plan and power the study for it. Only 4% of 929 comparisons in open-data psychology articles had been tested; invariance failed completely in 58% of new tests. (Maassen et al., 2023)
- [ ] **Relying on self-report when an objective measure of the behavior is available.** Pair self-report with records, biomarkers, logs or observed behavior where possible. Linked administrative data and laboratory validations show misreporting that is large and systematic rather than random. (Celhay et al., 2022; Adams et al., 2005; Manzke et al., 2025)
- [ ] **Treating the response rate as the only quality indicator.** Plan and report coverage, sampling, nonresponse and measurement error separately. A high response rate does not protect against the other three, and a low one is less damaging than was once assumed. (Shelley et al., 2021; Wetzel, 2010; Krosnick, 1999)
- [ ] **Measuring predictor and outcome with the same self-report method, in the same session, with overlapping items.** Keep items for different constructs distinct, separate the measurement of predictors and outcomes in time or method where feasible, and state in the paper why self-report is appropriate. Reviewers should expect design remedies, not post hoc statistical corrections. (Conway et al., 2010; Polas, 2025)

## Part B. Writing the questions

- [ ] **\* Double-barreled questions that ask two things and allow one answer.** One idea per item. This is among the most frequently found defects in cognitive interviews and in bias catalogs. (Silva et al., 2019; Choi et al., 2004)
- [ ] **Double negatives and negated items.** Phrase items in the positive. Negated versions carry less trait variance and produce separate wording factors in factor analysis. (Silva et al., 2019; Schriesheim et al., 1995)
- [ ] **\* Abstract or undefined terms.** Define the term in the item or give examples. Respondents interpret a phrase such as health professional in widely different ways, and precise technical terms are misread as well. (Willis et al., 2013; Collins, 2003)
- [ ] **Technical vocabulary assumed to be common knowledge.** Use plain alternatives and test them. Terms as ordinary as breastfeeding and antenatal care were misunderstood in cognitive interviews. (Ashok et al., 2021)
- [ ] **Questions that carry three or more concepts.** Split them. Respondents could not retain or answer long questions with several key concepts. (Ashok et al., 2021)
- [ ] **Recall periods respondents cannot anchor, such as in the last six months.** Anchor to life stages or salient events where the design allows, keep recall periods short, and expect error to rise with the time elapsed since the event. Forward and backward telescoping are both documented. (Ashok et al., 2021; Celhay et al., 2022)
- [ ] **Asking for counts of past behavior from unaided memory.** Provide lists, images or category prompts. Immediate self-reports of a just-completed shopping task were 44% accurate without cues and 78% to 89% accurate with image or text memory aids. (Manzke et al., 2025)
- [ ] **Questions that assume a condition which may not hold for the respondent.** Use a filter question first. Precondition binds were one of the five recurring comprehension problems found in cognitive interviews. (Silva et al., 2019)
- [ ] **Wording that violates the norms of ordinary conversation.** Respondents read questions as conversational turns. Loaded or unbalanced wording, and question forms that break those norms, introduce error. (Krosnick, 1999; Kalton et al., 1982)
- [ ] **Announcing the study's target behavior in instructions or item wording.** Keep instructions non-directive. A directive instruction that named the study focus produced overreporting and reactivity; a non-directive version produced unbiased reports. (Brenner et al., 2016)
- [ ] **\* Sensitive behavior items with no plan for socially desirable responding.** Self-administration and anonymity reduce the bias but do not remove it. High impression managers reported 20% to 33% less alcohol consumption and 30% to 50% fewer harms. Include a social desirability measure when the topic is sensitive, and consider a behavior-specific indicator, since person-level scales explain little variance in some domains. (Davis et al., 2010; King, 2022; Zhu et al., 2024; Vesely et al., 2020)
- [ ] **Items drawn from a bias catalog check never run.** Run the draft against a catalog of questionnaire biases before pretesting. One catalog lists 48 types with an example of each, grouped by question design, questionnaire design and administration. (Choi et al., 2004)

## Part C. Choosing response formats

- [ ] **\* Two-, three- or four-point rating scales.** Use five to seven points. Reliability, validity and discriminating power are poor below five points, improve up to about seven, and show no gain beyond six. (Preston et al., 2000; Lozano et al., 2008; Simms et al., 2019)
- [ ] **Very long scales (more than ten points).** Test-retest reliability falls beyond ten points. Respondents like ten-point scales but their answers are less stable. (Preston et al., 2000)
- [ ] **\* Labeling only the end points.** Label every point. Fully labeled scales give higher test-retest reliability, and labeling changes acquiescence and extreme responding. (Weng, 2004; Weijters et al., 2010)
- [ ] **\* Agree-disagree statements as the default format.** Write item-specific response options (for example, how often, how satisfied). Agree-disagree items invite acquiescence and gave much lower measurement quality in a multitrait-multimethod experiment. If they cannot be avoided, use five categories rather than seven or eleven. (Saris et al., 2010; Revilla et al., 2014)
- [ ] **Deciding on the midpoint by habit.** Offer a neutral option on obscure or low-salience topics, where respondents may have no basis to choose. Consider withholding it on controversial or sensitive items, where a minority use it as an escape. Most respondents use it validly, and including it slightly improved reliability. (Kankaraš et al., 2024; Johns, 2005)
- [ ] **\* Mixing positively and negatively worded items in one scale to catch acquiescence.** Prefer a version with all items in the same direction. Mixed versions lowered reliability and unidimensionality and reduced score variance, and reverse-coded items are the ones most damaged by careless responding. If reversed items are kept, model the wording factor rather than ignoring it. (Suárez-Álvarez et al., 2018; García-Fernández et al., 2022; Ertuna et al., 2026)
- [ ] **Don't know or not applicable options placed in line with the substantive options.** Separate them with a space or line. When they are not visually distinct, respondents misjudge the midpoint of the scale. (Tourangeau et al., 2004)
- [ ] **Response spacing or order that puts the visual midpoint away from the conceptual midpoint.** Space options evenly, keep them in a logical progression, and align the visual and conceptual midpoints. Answers shift toward the visual midpoint when the two differ. (Tourangeau et al., 2004; Christian et al., 2009)
- [ ] **Sliders, date-picker wheels, long horizontal scales and open text fields for respondents on phones.** Use vertical layouts with few options and keep open text to a minimum. Smartphone respondents gave answers as thoughtful as PC respondents but could not set sliders and date wheels accurately, and wrote shorter open answers. (Antoun et al., 2017; de Bruijne et al., 2014; Mavletova, 2013)
- [ ] **Choosing a format without checking which of its characteristics are known to matter.** Consult the classification of response-scale characteristics, which separates those shown to affect data quality from those shown not to and those still untested, and pretest the response options themselves. (DeCastellarnau, 2017; Abulela et al., 2024)

## Part D. Assembling the questionnaire: length, order, layout and device

- [ ] **\* An instrument that is longer than it needs to be.** Cut. Items placed later are answered faster, with more item nonresponse, shorter open answers and less variability, and eye tracking shows attention is lower for the same item at the end. (Galesic et al., 2009; Neuert, 2021)
- [ ] **Announcing a long completion time.** State the length honestly and keep it short. The longer the stated length, the fewer respondents started and finished. (Galesic et al., 2009)
- [ ] **A long instrument that cannot be cut.** Use a split questionnaire design: modularize, randomly assign subsets of modules, and impute the rest. Measurement error was lower for late questions and the imputation variance did not outweigh the gain. (Peytchev et al., 2017)
- [ ] **\* The same item order for every respondent.** Randomize blocks and, where items are unordered, the response options. Ratings drifted lower with reduced variance for tasks presented later, and primacy effects were strongest among fast respondents. (Berenbon et al., 2024; Malhotra, 2008)
- [ ] **A priming or sensitive question placed just before the target item.** Separate them. Placing a stereotype-evoking question right before a target changed attitudes significantly when items were on separate pages. (Stefkovics et al., 2022)
- [ ] **Grids and item-by-item pages mixed within one instrument.** Pick one format. The two were affected differently by question order, so mixing them creates measurement inequivalence. (Stefkovics et al., 2022)
- [ ] **Large, cognitively demanding grids.** Split them into single items. This reduced nondifferentiation and nonsubstantive answers, at the cost of longer completion time. (Roßmann et al., 2018)
- [ ] **Related items spread across several screens when a scale score is wanted, or crammed onto one screen when independent judgments are wanted.** Items on the same screen are intercorrelated more highly than items on separate screens. Place items according to which you want. (Tourangeau et al., 2004)
- [ ] **\* Designing for a desktop screen when many respondents will use a phone.** Build mobile-first. A mobile-first layout reduced item nonresponse without raising breakoffs or satisficing, and scrolling through grids is the main reason surveys take longer on phones. (Schwerdtfeger et al., 2026; Couper et al., 2017)
- [ ] **Changing visual layout or mode partway through a study.** Hold layout constant across modes and waves. Identically worded questions produced different answers when the visual layout changed. (Dillman et al., 2005; Dillman et al., 2007)
- [ ] **Assuming effects found in one mode will appear in another.** Order, context and response-order effects, retrospective accuracy and socially desirable responding all depend on administration mode; wording and question-form effects do not. (Schwarz et al., 1991; Ayidiya et al., 1990)

## Part E. Pretesting

- [ ] **\* No pretest, or a pretest that consists of colleagues reading the draft.** Run cognitive interviews with members of the target population, using think-aloud and verbal probing on comprehension, retrieval, judgment and response. Standardization assumes that respondents understand the questions in the same way; that assumption has to be checked. (Collins, 2003; Beatty et al., 2007; Willis et al., 2013)
- [ ] **\* One round of cognitive interviews, then fielding.** Test the revised items again. In a 2,200-person web experiment, cognitive interviewing identified real problems, but the revised question was not more valid than the original. (Lenzner et al., 2022)
- [ ] **Ten interviews when a larger validation is affordable.** Add web probing or a standardized response-process evaluation. Web probing with 508 online respondents found the same problems and the same revisions as 20 face-to-face interviews, and the response-process-evaluation method produces an item report with the percentage of respondents who interpreted each item as intended. (Lenzner et al., 2017; Wolf et al., 2025)
- [ ] **Expert review done informally by one person.** Use a structured appraisal scheme with more than one independent reviewer and compare their coding. Informal reviews rarely agree with each other. (Ikart, 2019)
- [ ] **Pretesting the wording but not the response options or instructions.** Probe the response options and the instructions as well. Ambiguous terms, technical terms and unclear instructions were the three recurring problems in a national survey pretest, and response options are interpreted as variably as stems. (Abulela et al., 2024; Ashok et al., 2021)
- [ ] **Translated or cross-cultural instruments fielded without cognitive testing in the target population.** Run cognitive interviews in each language and setting. Even a small number of interviews by interviewers with limited training found many problems in low-resource settings. (Scott et al., 2021; Ashok et al., 2021)
- [ ] **Pretesting under lab conditions when the survey will be administered in clinics, homes or care settings.** Pilot in the real setting. Completion there depended on environmental constraints, institutional mediation and trust rather than on the autonomous respondent assumed by the design literature. (Zhang et al., 2026)
- [ ] **Leaving the pretest out of the paper.** Report the pretest method, sample, problems found and changes made. Most published surveys do not say whether they were pretested at all. (Ikart, 2019; Shelley et al., 2021)

## Part F. Sampling and recruiting

- [ ] **\* Reporting prevalence or means from a convenience sample as population estimates.** Use student pools and online panels to test experimental and correlational hypotheses, not to estimate how common something is. Treatment effects from convenience samples closely matched population-based samples; prevalence estimates did not. (Mullinix et al., 2015; Jeong et al., 2018; Berinsky et al., 2012)
- [ ] **\* Treating the undergraduate pool and an online panel as interchangeable.** Decide which population the hypothesis is about and sample it. College students, MTurk workers and MTurk college students differed on every variable tested, and adding demographic covariates did not remove the differences. Student behavior offered bounds on other populations, and non-student samples were noisier. (Weigold et al., 2021; Snowberg et al., 2018; Goodman et al., 2013)
- [ ] **Describing a crowdsourced sample as non-clinical without checking.** Screen for clinical symptoms if they matter to the hypothesis. MTurk participants exceeded clinical cutoffs at 3 to 19 times the estimated 12-month prevalence. (Arditte et al., 2016)
- [ ] **Choosing a platform by habit or price alone.** Prolific and CloudResearch (with its quality filters on) gave the highest data quality and lowest cost per usable respondent in head-to-head comparisons; MTurk was low even with filters, and the undergraduate pool was free but slowest. The ranking is disputed by one platform's authors, so read both sides. (Douglas et al., 2023; Peer et al., 2021; Litman et al., 2021)
- [ ] **Using approval ratings as the quality filter.** Reputation scores did not predict data quality; how often and why people use the platform did. (Peer et al., 2021)
- [ ] **Fielding online without screeners.** Add selection screeners (does the respondent belong to the target population) and accuracy screeners (is the respondent attending), and report which were used. Every online source needed screening, and rigorous exclusion raised statistical power without biasing the remaining sample. (Arndt et al., 2021; Thomas et al., 2017)
- [ ] **Assuming a platform's quality is stable over time.** Check data quality in every wave. MTurk quality dropped sharply in summer 2018, with failed replications of established findings until screening was applied. (Chmielewski et al., 2019)
- [ ] **Running the same experiment on participants who have seen it before.** Non-naivety is a known problem on heavily used platforms; aggregated panels and newer platforms offered more naive and more diverse participants. (Peer et al., 2017; Chandler et al., 2019)
- [ ] **\* Public social media recruitment with an advertised incentive.** Use a two-stage design with a screener and personalized links, conceal eligibility criteria, avoid advertising the incentive, and avoid public groups. Exclusions for fraud ranged from 3% to 94% of responses across studies, and higher incentives attracted more fraud. (Comachio et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2024; Griffin et al., 2021)
- [ ] **Collecting from the participant pool in the last weeks of the semester.** Field earlier if the design allows. Careless responding was more prevalent near the end of the semester. (Braitman et al., 2025)

## Part G. Preventing and detecting low-quality responses

- [ ] **\* No data-quality evaluation planned at all.** Decide before fielding which indicators will be collected and which rules will be applied. In a census of 3,298 online survey articles from 2022, 55% reported no data-quality evaluation and 24% used only one method. (Gottfried, 2024)
- [ ] **Writing a difficult survey and hoping respondents will try hard.** Reduce task difficulty first. Satisficing rises when the task is hard and motivation or ability is low; easing the task is the most effective lever, followed by making the request worth the respondent's time. (Krosnick, 1999; Artino et al., 2022)
- [ ] **\* Relying on a single attention check.** Place several short, covert checks through the survey and combine them with response-time and consistency indices. Long instructed manipulation checks invite noncompliance, and single-item screening risks both keeping careless cases and dropping attentive ones. (Muszyński, 2023; Ward et al., 2022; Curran, 2016)
- [ ] **\* Dropping everyone who fails one check.** Preregister the exclusion rule. Retaining participants who failed only one of four checks had minimal effect on data quality while preserving sample diversity, exclusion changed sample demographics, and attrition from checks has reached 70% in some samples. (Braitman et al., 2025; Abbey et al., 2017)
- [ ] **Longstring (straightlining) as the only post hoc index.** Use response time per item, psychometric antonyms or synonyms, individual reliability and Mahalanobis distance. Longstring and intra-individual variability failed to detect induced careless responding in an experiment. (Goldammer et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2012)
- [ ] **Anonymous, obligatory participation with no detection items.** Prefer identified responses where ethics allow, insert instructed response items before data collection, and compute consistency indices afterwards. About 10% to 12% of undergraduates completing a long survey for credit responded carelessly. (Meade et al., 2012)
- [ ] **Warning messages used to raise attention on sensitive surveys.** Warnings can increase socially desirable answering. Detection items, by contrast, had a motivating rather than demotivating effect in one experiment. (Clifford et al., 2015; Shamon et al., 2020)
- [ ] **Ignoring speeders.** Flag respondents averaging under about ten seconds per question; their accuracy on verifiable questions was lower. (Kato et al., 2021)
- [ ] **\* Treating careless data as harmless noise.** Unremoved careless responses can inflate correlations, create spurious factors and ruin model fit; three high-profile data sets would have shown meaningfully larger effects without screening. (Stosic et al., 2024; Arias et al., 2020)
- [ ] **No defenses against fraudulent and automated responses.** Use platform bot detection, duplicate IP and e-mail checks, timing and geolocation comparisons, consistency questions and open-ended items, and review the data during collection rather than only afterwards. Most low-quality online data traced back to fraudulent human users outside the target country rather than to bots, which changes which defenses matter. (Ng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023; Pinzón et al., 2024; Jaffe et al., 2026)
- [ ] **Leaving the screening out of the paper.** Report the detection methods, cutoffs, number removed and results with and without the removed cases. Practice across 17 management journals was unstandardized and poorly reported. (Huang et al., 2025; Ward et al., 2022)

## Part H. Analysis decisions that start at the design stage

- [ ] **\* Parametric tests on single Likert items.** Analyze summed multi-item scales, where parametric tests hold up well, and use ordinal methods for single items. Item-by-item analysis is the practice that the measurement literature asks to stop. (Carifio et al., 2007; Sullivan et al., 2013)
- [ ] **Pearson correlations or standard factor analysis on ordinal items where one category dominates.** Use polychoric correlations and an appropriate estimator. Pearson estimates are biased toward zero in that situation, even with more than ten categories. (Verhulst et al., 2021)
- [ ] **Dichotomizing a Likert outcome, or treating a skewed one as continuous.** Use proportional odds or win probability methods; they had higher power and lower bias in simulations. (Al-Jaishi et al., 2026)
- [ ] **Reporting Cronbach's alpha as the only evidence of measurement quality.** Report reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity against several criteria, with their sampling error. Alpha alone is usually inadequate. (Cheung et al., 2023)
- [ ] **Reverse-coding negatively worded items and assuming equal intervals.** Expect a method factor. That combination manufactured an artifactual second factor in a widely used self-control scale and changed its correlations with outcomes. (Paap et al., 2023)
- [ ] **Ignoring response styles when scoring.** Where acquiescence or social desirability is plausible, model it. Ignoring both gave the worst validity evidence; item response models and Thurstonian models can separate response bias from the construct. (Kreitchmann et al., 2019; Grimmond et al., 2025; Ulitzsch et al., 2022)
- [ ] **Post hoc statistical fixes for common method bias.** Design remedies first (distinct items, separation in time or method). Post hoc statistical controls have significant drawbacks and some have performed poorly. (Conway et al., 2010)
- [ ] **Omitting the item details a replication would need.** Report the exact item wording, response labels, number of points and midpoint for every scale. Reviews find these details missing in a large share of evaluations. (South et al., 2022; Flake et al., 2020)

## If you read only seven papers

In this order. The first gives the theory, the next four cover design, data quality, scale format and measurement, the sixth covers recruitment, and the last shows where the evidence on pretesting runs out.

1. [Survey research](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.50.1.537) (Krosnick, 1999). One theory, satisficing, that explains most of the mistakes in Parts B to D. Read the sections on satisficing and on scale labels.
2. A Catalog of Biases in Questionnaires (Choi et al., 2004). The 48-item bias catalog. Use the table as an audit of a draft, not as reading.
3. [Dealing with Careless Responding in Survey Data: Prevention, Identification, and Recommended Best Practices](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-040422-045007) (Ward et al., 2022). The current review of careless responding, with screening recommendations by level of stringency.
4. [A classification of response scale characteristics that affect data quality: a literature review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-017-0533-4) (DeCastellarnau, 2017). Which response-scale decisions are known to matter, known not to matter, and still untested.
5. [Measurement Schmeasurement: Questionable Measurement Practices and How to Avoid Them](https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245920952393) (Flake et al., 2020). The questions to ask about any measure before using it, and the case for reporting measurement decisions.
6. [Data quality in online human-subjects research: Comparisons between MTurk, Prolific, CloudResearch, Qualtrics, and SONA](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0279720) (Douglas et al., 2023). Five recruitment sources compared, including an undergraduate pool, with cost per usable respondent. Read with the Peer et al. and Litman et al. exchange.
7. [An experimental test of the effectiveness of cognitive interviewing in pretesting questionnaires](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-022-01489-4) (Lenzner et al., 2022). The evidence that pretesting finds problems but does not by itself fix them. It is the reason Part E asks for a second round.
